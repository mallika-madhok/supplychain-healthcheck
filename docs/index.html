Methodology

        
What the numbers mean, where they come from, and what they can't tell you.


        
What this is for

        
Three things you don't control can move your costs: how hard it is to ship goods
        around the world, the Canadian dollar against the US dollar, and how much of your
        buying depends on a few countries. This page puts a plain reading on each, translates
        the currency one into dollars, and turns each reading into a concrete next step. It does not forecast
        your costs, and it says nothing about any specific supplier or brand.


        
What you enter

        
The product you mostly buy, your monthly purchases, the share of those purchases
        paid in US dollars, and roughly what share comes from each country. These are
        estimates you give, not data we collect, and they stay in your browser and the page
        address.


        
How the status labels are decided

        
Each signal is compared with its own history and expressed as a z-score:
        how far today's value sits from its average, counted in standard deviations
        (the size of a typical swing for that signal). Around 0 is average, up to 1 is a normal
        swing, 1 to 2 is unusual, 2 to 3 is rare, and 3 or more means something is really off.
        Below 1 is shown as Normal, 1 to 2 as Worse than usual, and above 2 as
        Much worse than usual. These cut-offs are my first-pass choice and haven't been tested
        against real price changes.


        
The three signals

        
    
          
  • Shipping. The New York Fed's Global Supply Chain Pressure Index, compared
          with its own history since 1998. We also show how today ranks against every month
          since then, and whether it has been rising or easing over the last three months. This
          is global, so it is the same for every business.
    • 
          
  • Currency. Two measures of the US/Canadian dollar rate, each compared with
          history since 2017 and averaged for the status: how far the rate has moved over the
          last 30 trading days (about six weeks), and how jumpy it has been over the last 90
          (about four months). The dollar figures are simple arithmetic on your inputs:
          your USD purchases are your monthly purchases times your USD share, and a rate
          move of X% changes what those purchases cost in Canadian dollars by X%. "A typical
          swing" is the standard deviation of the six-week move since 2017.
    • 
          
  • Suppliers. A concentration score for your country mix: square each country's
          share and add them up (a single country at 100% scores 1, an even spread scores
          close to 0). It is compared with the same score for Canada's imports across all
          clothing and footwear product lines over the last 12 months. "Other" is treated as
          spread across many suppliers, so it adds nothing. The suggested change moves 10
          points (or a third of your top country's share, if smaller) to the largest Canadian
          import source you rely on least.
    •

Limits to keep in mind

        
    
          
  • Dollar figures assume your purchase volume stays the same and that you pay spot
          rates. If you hedge, or your supplier changes its prices when the rate moves, your
          real numbers will differ.
    • 
          
  • Country means where customs says the goods were made, which is not always where
          they were sewn.
    • 
          
  • About 0.0001% of import value has no country recorded and is left out of the
          concentration figures.
    • 
          
  • Past history is a guide to what is unusual, not a forecast. This is not financial
          advice.
    • 
        


        
How this page works

        
There is no server. A weekly job downloads the latest data, recalculates it and
        publishes small data files next to this page. Your browser loads them with
        DuckDB-Wasm
        and runs the SQL you can open under each chart, right on your device.
